\subsection{Software and Tools}

All code is written in Python, the standard language of medical AI research. PyTorch was chosen because the whole chain (projector, SIRT, diffusion network, detector and Gaussian fitting) needs automatic differentiation on the GPU in one framework. Instead of an external tomography toolbox we wrote our own ray-driven DTS projector in PyTorch: it reproduces the exact linear-sweep geometry, provides gradients for the Gaussian refinement, and needs no separate CUDA build, which had caused installation problems with toolbox-based setups. LIDC-IDRI is read with pydicom and pylidc, the official tools for its annotations; conformal predictive systems use the peer-reviewed crepes library; and the application uses Gradio, which builds a browser interface directly from Python.  \Cref{tab:tools} lists the tools with the alternatives considered.

\subsection{Configuration and Pipeline}
All of these settings (geometry, dose, prism size, network size, sampling steps, number of hypotheses, $\alpha=0.1$) are contained in one YAML file and the random seed is fixed. There are 18 steps in the pipeline: 00 environment check, 01 physics verification, 02 patient selection, 03 downloading TCIA, 04 splitting cases, 05 DTS simulation, 06 SIRT simulation, 07 training pairs, 08 diffusion training, 09 per-nodule pipeline, 10 evaluation \& conformal intervals, 11 growth, 12 demo, 13 geometry sensitivity, 14 detector, 15 fit check, 16 conformal predictive systems, 17 explainability and 18 HTML report. With each step, PASS/FAIL lines are printed, along with the expected value.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}

\paragraph{Source.} The Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Database Resource Initiative (LIDC-IDRI) collection~\cite{armato2011lidc} was used, which was distributed by The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) under the Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 licence. 1,018 thoracic CT scans from 1,010 patients was collected at seven academic centres and eight medical imaging companies from various manufacturers. Four expert thoracic radiologists independently reviewed each scan twice -- without using a ruler, and with the other radiologists' markings. Each nodule 3 mm or larger was marked and each reader assessed nodule characteristics including malignancy. The only large public CT collection with nodule outlines by multiple radiologists is LIDC-IDRI, and this is precisely what is required as both a 3D ground truth and to assess inter-reader variability. A public dataset where chest DTS were paired with CT could not be found and therefore DTS were simulated from these CT scans.

\paragraph{Selection.} Patients were selected with pylidc~\cite{hancock2016pylidc} (\cref{fig:dataset}): a scan was kept if its slice thickness was at most 2.5~mm and it contained at least one nodule of 4--30~mm marked by at least three of the four radiologists, the convention used by LUNA16~\cite{setio2017luna}. This gave 583 patients with 1{,}144 nodules. Annotations from different readers were grouped into nodules by pylidc's clustering, and a nodule's \emph{consensus mask} is the set of voxels outlined by at least 50\% of its readers; the individual reader masks were kept to measure inter-reader variability.

\paragraph{Split.} Patients, not nodules, were split 60/20/20 into training (350), calibration (117) and test (116) sets, so that no patient contributes nodules to two sets. The test set contains 225 nodules. The diffusion prior and detector were trained on the training patients, with 10\% of them held out to select the diffusion checkpoint; the calibration patients were used to select the detector checkpoint and to calibrate the conformal methods; the test patients were used only for the final results.

\paragraph{Preprocessing.} About 64~GB of DICOM was downloaded from TCIA and converted into one compact case file per scan: the CT in Hounsfield units (clipped to $[-1024, 3071]$ and stored as 16-bit integers), the consensus and reader masks of every nodule (bit-packed), and a header with the voxel geometry and patient ID, after which the DICOM was deleted. The CT table was removed by keeping the largest connected body component, because a table would not be present in a chest DTS acquisition. We verified that the DICOM slice positions agree with pylidc's for every scan (difference 0.000~mm), that the consensus masks lie on nodule tissue (median $-202$~HU) and that the consensus volume is on average 0.81 of the mean reader volume.

\paragraph{DTS simulation and training pairs.} For each case, 15 projections over $\pm15^\circ$ were simulated at CT resolution with Poisson noise ($I_0 = 2\times10^4$ photons per pixel and view; median single-view nodule contrast-to-noise ratio 3.3) and reconstructed by whole-chest SIRT. Training pairs for the diffusion prior were cut from the training patients only: for each pair, the prism SIRT of the DTS is the condition and the same prism of the CT is the target, taken around nodules and around nodule-free lung regions so that the prior also learns normal anatomy. This gave \num{2522} training pairs and 265 held-out pairs from the training patients; the condition kept the prism's attenuation (median ratio to CT 0.956, 10--90\%: 0.85--1.03), and its PSNR against the CT target was 10.66~dB, the baseline the prior must beat. \Cref{fig:dtsviews} shows the simulated views and the SIRT reconstruction of one patient and \cref{fig:pair} one training pair.

\section{Testing and Evaluation}

This section outlines the testing of the system, data division, metrics/baselines, and verification of all requirements and discovery of and resolution to problems.

\subsection{Verification Strategy}
 There were four levels of testing:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Unit tests (69):} projector adjointness, mass conservation, conformal coverage (synthetic data), Mondrian group coverage, CPS probabilities (against crepes), CGLS seen/unseen split, and fit-check parsing.\item \textbf{Smoke test:} entire pipeline on synthetic chest phantoms on CPU in 10-20 minutes.\item \textbf{Real-data pilots:} CPU (4 patients) and Kaggle (40 patients) which uncovered and patched the depth-support bug (prism attenuation 67--81\% $\rightarrow$ 98.7\%).\item \textbf{Full run:} 583 patients with automatic checks at each step and HTML report which contains all checks.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data Split and Evaluation Protocol}
 Patients, not nodules, were split 60/20/20: 350 training, 117 calibration and 116 test patients. The diffusion model was selected on 10\% of the training patients held out, the detector on the calibration patients, and the conformal methods were calibrated on the calibration patients. Test patients were used only for the final tables.

\subsection{Metrics and Baselines}
 Volume and mass error (median signed error, median absolute percentage error MdAPE, Bland--Altman limits), failure rate, nodule-box PSNR, interval coverage and median width, Brier score and expected calibration error (ECE) of category probabilities, doubling-time error and NELSON category accuracy, detection CPM and sensitivity, seen fraction and unseen skill, and run time. Baselines: 2D diameter converted to a sphere volume (clinical practice), coarse SIRT, prism ROI-SIRT, physics-only Gaussians, and diffusion without Gaussians.

\subsection{Issues Found and Fixed}
 (1) Without a depth support, SIRT placed attenuation in the air around the patient and the prism lost a third of its attenuation as false background; fixed by confining SIRT to the patient. (2) The training-free detector mostly found end-on vessels (recall in the top 10 of 6\% in the pilot); replaced by the learned detector. (3) A first CPS implementation capped probabilities at $n/(n+1)$; fixed with the smoothed mid-point p-value and verified against crepes. (4) In step~10 the asymmetric split-conformal upper bound for some methods became effectively unbounded because calibration nodules where a method found nothing (measured volume $\approx 0$) produce unbounded log-ratios. This is not fixed in the reported results; its effect on the interval widths is analysed in \cref{sec:resultsdisc}, and the fix (calibrating only measurable cases) is listed as future work. (5) Three checks of the full run failed and are reported, not hidden: the H1 and H2 checks of step~10 (the hypotheses were not supported) and the virtual-growth construction check of step~11 (7.5\% deviation against a 3\% limit, caused by vessels near the nodule inside the mass window).

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:resultsdisc}

The results are presented in the order of the pipeline: the training and fit of the diffusion prior, nodule detection, measurement accuracy (H1, H2), calibrated uncertainty (H3, H5), growth (H4) and explainability (H6), followed by a discussion of why the measurements failed, the sensitivity to the acquisition geometry, and the application. The measurement results use 150 nodules (76 calibration, 74 test); detection uses all 225 test nodules.

\subsection{Diffusion Prior Training and Fit}

The prior was trained on \num{2522} pairs (265 held out) for \num{27610} steps (batch 8); the loss fell from 0.1186 to 0.0476 (last 10\% vs first 10\% of steps). On held-out pairs its samples reached 15.23~dB PSNR in the nodule box against 11.88~dB for the physics-only input (+3.35~dB) and 13.74 vs 10.16~dB over the whole prism (\cref{fig:sample}). The fit check (\cref{fig:fit}) gives two messages. The denoising loss at fixed noise levels was nearly the same on training and held-out pairs (0.0342 vs 0.0331, ratio 0.97), so the network did not memorise its inputs; but the sample quality gain was larger on training patients than on held-out ones (+7.00 vs +4.22~dB), so its samples are better for patients it has seen. The held-out gain was still rising at the end (+1.89 to +3.35~dB over the last checks), so the prior is also under-trained.

\textbf{H1 (mass error $<$ volume error) is not supported.} Mass had a lower MdAPE than volume only for the physics-only Gaussians (78.0\% vs 87.7\%); for the other methods it was equal or worse. \textbf{H2 (the prior reduces volume error) is not supported for the full method}: diffusion with Gaussians had a volume MdAPE of 94.6\% against 87.7--91.2\% for the physics-only methods. The diffusion prior alone had the lowest volume MdAPE (74.5\%) and the fewest failures (3\%), but a strong negative bias ($-58\%$). No method reached clinically useful accuracy: even the best had only 14\% of nodules within $\pm$20\%, and the clinical 2D baseline had an MdAPE of 88\%. For comparison, the radiologists' own volumes differ by a median coefficient of variation of 15\%.

Two observations explain these numbers. First, image quality and measurement accuracy disagree: the diffusion prior raised the nodule-box PSNR from about 12~dB to 17.49~dB, yet its volumes are the most biased. Second, all learned methods \emph{under}-estimate: \cref{fig:volerr,fig:scatter} show most diffusion and Gaussian estimates far below the line of identity, and \cref{fig:ba} shows that the full method's errors cluster at $-100\%$ (nodules shrunk or erased) with a few large over-estimates. \Cref{fig:h1} shows the error distributions of volume and mass side by side and the volume error against nodule size: errors are large at every size.

\subsection{Calibrated Uncertainty (H3, H5)}

\Cref{tab:uq} gives the conformal intervals of the full method. All variants calibrated on the 76 calibration nodules reach or exceed the 90\% target coverage on the test nodules except the Mondrian variant for volume, so the coverage guarantee of conformal prediction held in practice (\cref{fig:coverage}). The raw ensemble spread, used as $\pm z$ standard deviations, covered only 28\% at the 90\% level. \textbf{However, the intervals are not informative.} Their median width is many orders of magnitude larger than the estimate, and the normalised variants are effectively unbounded. This is because failures: if a measurement is zero then the score is infinite, and with 22--26\% failure on the calibration nodules the conformal quantile score is infinite. The only exception is the Mondrian variant which has finite widths, at an estimated 58 times. H3 thus has validity (coverage) but not usefulness (width) and the sub-claim that mass intervals are narrower than volume intervals can be determined only with bounded widths. Both results are shown for each test nodule (\cref{fig:intervals}): the truth is contained within 70 of the 74 intervals; however, 61 upper bounds of the intervals are above $10^5$ mm$^3$ and 16 of the failed reads (estimate zero) are at the bottom of their intervals.

\subsection{Why the Measurements Failed}

The results point to three causes, each of which suggests a fix.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The prior shrinks faint nodules.} Training pairs were cut around nodules and around nodule-free lung, and the prior was not told that a nodule is present at the click. For faint nodules it often produces plausible normal lung instead, which is consistent with the strong negative bias, the larger errors of low-density nodules and the low ensemble spread on failures. \Cref{fig:example} shows such a case. A fix is to condition the prior on the click or oversample nodule pairs.
  \item \textbf{Mass is measured in image space.} The X-rays fully define the mass (0.3\% unseen), but our measurement is done by integrating the reconstruction in a segmented window, so there is a leakage from segmentation failures and from shrinkage of the prior, into the mass. As intended by the H1 physics, a projection-domain mass estimate would be an integration of the detector signal itself.\item \textbf{Failures break the calibration.} The log-errors for measurements of zero are infinite and intervals are unbounded. Reporting ``not measurable'' as a different outcome with its own probability and calibrating only those measurable would result in finite intervals.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Acquisition Geometry Sensitivity}

On 12 nodules of two test patients, the depth smear (depth over in-plane width of the artifact spread) was 5.13 for 15 views over \ang{30}, 5.34 for 60 views over \ang{30} (four times the compute), 4.69 for 15 views over \ang{40} and 4.61 for 74 views over \ang{40}, against 0.95 for CT. At equal dose the number of views hardly matters, while a \ang{40} sweep reduces the smear by about 9\%; our 15-view, \ang{30} setting is therefore the conservative case.

\subsection{Application}

Measuring one nodule with all five reconstruction methods took a median of 45.5~s. The application loads the trained models on a free Kaggle notebook and is reached from a browser through an HTTPS tunnel. For a click on an annotated nodule it returns mass and volume with intervals, the EUPS category with probabilities, the prism views with the sample spread, and an interactive 3D view in which the reconstruction is coloured by the disagreement of the eight samples and the radiologists' nodule is shown in grey (\cref{fig:demo}); in both views the uncertainty is largest in depth, as the physics predicts. \section{Summary}

All requirements that had a fixed criterion were automatically checked. As expected, the pipeline, the simulation, the reconstruction, the detector and the uncertainty machinery behaved as designed: the learned detector outperformed the training-free baseline by 17x in CPM, the diffusion prior outperformed the held-out pairs with respect to nodule-region PSNR by 3.35 dB, conformal intervals achieved their target coverage, and the seen/unseen analysis demonstrated that the nodule's mass is completely captured by the X-rays and that the prior's "unseen" component correlates with the truth. None of the measurements in themselves achieved the objective -- H1 and H2 were not achieved and the intervals, though valid, were too large to be of use -- and our method did not achieve H4. These failures were attributed to the previous shrink of faint nodules, mass measurements in image space not from the projections, and failures in the measurement of nodules within the calibration set. These are not open-ended but concrete, testable fixes.
