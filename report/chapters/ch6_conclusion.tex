\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This chapter summarises what MedLift-3D achieved, states its limitations and future work, and reflects on the independent learning the project required.

\section{Summary}

MedLift-3D turns one click on a low-dose chest tomosynthesis image into a 3D nodule measurement with calibrated uncertainty. We showed from projection moments that DTS measures a nodule's mass in every view but its depth extent about 15 times worse, and built the system around that fact: a prism reconstruction around the click, a 17.4-million-parameter conditional diffusion prior kept consistent with the X-rays, 3D Gaussians that give mass in closed form, conformal intervals and calibrated size-category probabilities, a learned detector to suggest clicks, and a seen/unseen explanation of every result. A verified 18-step pipeline ran a 583-patient virtual clinical trial on LIDC-IDRI in 14 hours for about US\$16. The components worked as specified: the prior improved nodule-region PSNR from 11.88 to 15.23~dB on held-out data, the detector reached a CPM of 0.298 against 0.017 for a training-free baseline, conformal intervals reached their target coverage, calibrated probabilities lowered the Brier score for $P(V\geq100)$ from 0.54 to 0.17, and the null-space analysis showed that the X-rays measure almost all of a nodule's mass and that the prior's unseen content correlates with the truth. The measurements did not reach the goal: mass was not more accurate than volume (H1), the full method did not beat physics-only reconstruction (H2), MDT did not beat VDT for our method (H4), and the valid intervals were too wide to be useful (H3). Calibrated probabilities (H5) helped at one of two thresholds, and the prior's positive unseen skill supports H6. The analysis traced the failures to specific causes with specific fixes, and showed that better image quality did not mean better measurement, which is itself a lesson for medical generative AI.

\section{Limitation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulation only.} No public chest DTS dataset with CT ground truth exists; our DTS is simulated from CT with a monochromatic beam and no scatter, so performance on real scanners is unknown.
  \item \textbf{Acquisition.} 15 views instead of the 60--74 of clinical units (at equal dose) and a conservative \ang{30} sweep; the prior was trained and tested on this geometry only.
  \item \textbf{Detector sensitivity.} 35\% at two false positives per scan is too low for unaided use; the detector is an aid only.
  \item \textbf{Growth.} Virtual growth scales the nodule uniformly; real nodules grow irregularly.
  \item \textbf{Ground truth.} LIDC labels vary between radiologists (consensus volume / mean reader volume 0.81), and only nodules of 4--30~mm marked by at least three readers were used.
  \item \textbf{Population.} LIDC is mostly from US patients; populations with tuberculosis scarring were not represented.
  \item \textbf{Measurement accuracy.} Volume and mass errors (MdAPE 75--100\%) are far from clinical use; the full method under-estimates strongly (bias $-91\%$) and fails on 22\% of nodules.
  \item \textbf{Uninformative intervals.} Failed measurements inside the calibration set made the intervals valid but unbounded; the ensemble spread was negatively correlated with the error.
  \item \textbf{Training budget and size.} The prior trained for 6 hours and was still improving, and its samples were better for training patients than for new ones (+7.00 vs +4.22~dB); the detector also partly memorised its training patients.
  \item \textbf{Sample size.} Measurements used 150 nodules (76 calibration, 74 test) and 40 growth pairs, so small differences between methods are not conclusive.
  \item \textbf{Patient thickness} was taken from the CT body outline; a real DTS unit would have to measure it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Nodule-preserving prior:} condition the diffusion model on the click position and oversample nodule pairs, so that faint nodules are not replaced by normal lung.
  \item \textbf{Projection-domain mass:} estimate mass by integrating the detector signal around the nodule's shadow, which the null-space analysis shows is fully measured, instead of integrating a segmented reconstruction.
  \item \textbf{Failure-aware calibration:} report ``not measurable'' as a separate outcome with its own probability and calibrate intervals on measurable cases only, and replace the ensemble spread by a difficulty score that correlates positively with error.
  \item \textbf{Real DTS data:} partner with a hospital that has a DTS unit to collect paired DTS and CT scans, recalibrate the conformal layer on them, and measure real performance.
  \item \textbf{Physics realism:} add polychromatic spectra, scatter and the 60--74-view clinical protocols to the simulator.
  \item \textbf{Longer and larger training:} train the prior longer with augmentation, test larger models, and train the detector with more nodule-free negatives.
  \item \textbf{Real growth:} use longitudinal CT pairs instead of virtual growth.
  \item \textbf{Reader study:} measure whether radiologists make better follow-up decisions with MedLift-3D than with DTS alone.
  \item \textbf{Deployment:} a Bangla interface, DICOM/PACS integration, an on-site GPU option, and a quality system towards regulatory registration.
  \item \textbf{Other limited-angle problems:} breast tomosynthesis and dental cone-beam CT share the missing-wedge structure.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Independent Learning and Reflection}

The project required knowledge and skills well beyond our coursework. This section reflects on that independent learning under five aspects: curiosity, self-initiative, independence, transfer of past learning, and reflection on what we learned.

\subsection{Curiosity}
 The project began as a generative-modelling idea, but we kept asking a deeper question: what can a \ang{30} X-ray sweep actually measure? Following that question into projection moments led to the mass-first design. The same curiosity made us ask whether the prior recovers or invents depth, which became the seen/unseen analysis, and when we found that raw ensemble spread is not a probability we studied conformal prediction until we could produce calibrated category probabilities.

\subsection{Self-initiative}
 Beyond the required scope we added a learned detector after the training-free one mostly found vessels, a fit check to show that the prior does not overfit, conformal predictive systems with size-group calibration, explainability with a truth-referenced test, a geometry-sensitivity study to justify the 15-view choice, and an automatic HTML report so that every check is visible to examiners.

\subsection{Independence}
 Outside the classroom we learned to run a 14-hour unattended job on rented cloud machines (CPU and GPU pods sharing a network volume, terminal multiplexing, resumable scripts and backups), to download and organise 64~GB of medical images from TCIA, to read radiologist annotations with pylidc, and to serve a GPU application from a Kaggle notebook through an HTTPS tunnel when the usual share link failed.

\subsection{Transfer of Past Learning}
 Much of the project rests on knowledge from earlier courses applied to a problem none of them covered. Linear algebra became the tool for separating what the X-rays measure from what the prior supplies; calculus gave the moment argument that founded the design; probability and statistics became calibrated uncertainty; and software engineering became the discipline of a verified pipeline. \Cref{tab:transfer} lists how each area was applied.

\subsection{Reflection on Learning}
 Our main lesson was that a large architecture with falling training losses does not show that a single clinical measurement is right. Rebuilding the work around measurable clinical numbers, explicit hypotheses and automatic checks changed how we think about engineering: a result counts only if someone else can verify it, and a negative result reported honestly is worth more than a claim that cannot be tested. We also learned to work within real limits of time, money and data, to plan computation like a budget, and to treat uncertainty as part of the answer rather than an embarrassment. These habits, more than any single model, are what we take from the project into our careers.
