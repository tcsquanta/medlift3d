\chapter{Standards and Design Constraints}
\label{ch:standards}

This chapter lists the standards MedLift-3D follows, with the alternatives considered, and analyses its economic, environmental, ethical, health and safety, social, political, legal, cultural and sustainability aspects. It ends with the cost analysis and the complex engineering problem mapping.

\section{Compliance with the Standards}

MedLift-3D is research software intended to become medical-device software, so we followed the standards that such software must eventually meet, even where formal certification is out of scope for a student project. For each standard we state how it is applied, which alternatives were considered with their pros and cons, and why it was selected. The standards are grouped into software, hardware and communication standards.

\subsection{Software Standards}

The software standards cover data formats, the software life cycle, risk and quality management, code quality, numerical precision and licensing (\cref{tab:swstd}). DICOM is the format in which hospitals store and exchange images, so the input path reads DICOM directly and checks its geometry; intermediate data use compressed NumPy files with JSON headers because they hold each case's volume, masks and metadata in one self-describing file. IEC 62304 defines the life-cycle processes for medical device software; we are not certified, but we followed its core practices: every requirement is traced to a test, all code is under version control, 69 unit tests run automatically, and anomalies found during development are documented (\cref{ch:results}). ISO 14971 risk management is applied through the hazard analysis in \cref{tab:risk}, while ISO 13485 quality management is identified as necessary for a future product. IEEE 754 arithmetic is used deliberately: physics and measurement run in 32-bit precision and only the diffusion network uses bf16, which keeps the 32-bit range and avoids the overflow that fp16 causes in 3D networks. Finally, all data and libraries are used under open licences with attribution.

\subsection{Hardware Standards}

MedLift-3D is software, so the hardware standards concern the computing platform and the X-ray equipment that would produce real data (\cref{tab:hwstd}). All GPU work uses NVIDIA's CUDA platform through PyTorch, because CUDA GPUs are the ones offered by every cloud provider we could afford; AMD's ROCm platform is an open alternative but is rarely available for rent. The safety of the X-ray equipment itself is governed by IEC 60601-1 and its part 2-54 for radiography; these apply to the DTS unit, not to our software, and a clinical deployment would rely on a certified unit. Radiation doses are compared as effective dose following ICRP Publication 103, the standard measure of patient risk.

\subsection{Communication Standards}

The communication standards concern how the application is reached and how results are exchanged (\cref{tab:commstd}). The application runs as a web server and is reached over HTTP locally and over an encrypted HTTPS tunnel from outside, so any modern browser can use it without installing software; the usual Gradio share link failed on Kaggle, and SSH port forwarding would require setup on the client. Reports, results and calibration files are written as JSON, which is human-readable and language-neutral, unlike Python pickle files, which are unsafe to load from untrusted sources. Integration with hospital systems would use DICOM networking for images and HL7 FHIR for reports, the standards used by picture archiving and electronic record systems.

\section{Design Constraints}

The design was shaped by economic, environmental, ethical, health and safety, social, political, sustainability, legal and cultural constraints. Each is discussed below together with its effect on the design and the measures taken.

\subsection{Economic Constraint}

The project had no external funding, so all computation had to fit a student budget. Free resources were used wherever possible (laptop CPU, Kaggle GPUs for the pilot and the application), CPU-only steps (download and case building) were moved to a cheap CPU machine, and GPU training was given a fixed compute budget. Clinically, the economic case rests on DTS: a DTS-capable radiography room costs a fraction of a CT scanner and examines patients faster, so follow-up by DTS can lower the cost of each follow-up for hospitals and patients.

\subsection{Environmental Constraint}

\paragraph{Energy and emissions.} The L40S GPU has a 350~W power limit, and the whole study ran on this single GPU, so its energy use and emissions (at a grid intensity of about 0.48~kg CO$_2$ per kWh worldwide and about 0.6 in Bangladesh) were small. One measurement (about 45~s on the GPU) uses about 0.004~kWh. These are estimates from rated power, following~\cite{lannelongue2021green}, not metered values.

\paragraph{Data storage and waste.} The 64~GB of downloaded DICOM was deleted after conversion to compact cases, and intermediate files that can be regenerated were not backed up. No hardware was bought, so the project produced no electronic waste.

\paragraph{Radiation as an environmental factor.} Replacing CT follow-ups (about 1.5--6~mSv) with DTS (about 0.1~mSv) reduces the population's collective radiation dose.

\paragraph{Mitigations.} A small model (17.4~M parameters) instead of a large generator; a prism instead of whole-volume generation; fixed compute budgets; CPU work on CPU machines; trained models reused for evaluation and the application instead of retraining; and, for future runs, cloud regions powered by renewable energy.

\subsection{Ethical Constraint}

\paragraph{Professional ethics.} We followed the IEEE~\cite{ieeeethics} and ACM~\cite{acmethics} codes of ethics, in particular honesty about claims and results.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Honest claims:} all results come from \emph{simulated} DTS, not real scanners, and \cref{sec:gap} lists what we do not claim.
  \item \textbf{Honest results:} hypotheses were stated before the results, and the report gives whichever outcome we found, including that H1 and H2 were not supported, that the intervals were too wide to be useful, that the detector sensitivity is only 35\% at two false positives per scan, and the failed checks in \cref{ch:results}.
  \item \textbf{Credit:} all datasets, libraries and ideas we build on are cited, including the data citations required by TCIA.
  \item \textbf{Fairness:} LIDC-IDRI comes mainly from US patients; performance on other populations, for example patients with healed tuberculosis scars, is unknown and is stated as such.
  \item \textbf{Data ethics:} only de-identified public data, used under its licence, with no attempt at re-identification.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Professional responsibility.} The team owns its decisions and mistakes. When the real-data pilot showed that the prism lost a third of its attenuation, we traced the cause to our own missing depth support and fixed it before the full run; when the first design of the project could not be verified, we narrowed it rather than present training losses as success. Each step's owner (\cref{tab:tasks}) is accountable for its checks. The system is decision support: the responsibility for a diagnosis stays with the radiologist.

\subsection{Health and Safety Constraint}

\paragraph{Health impacts.} \emph{Physical:} the main benefit is lower radiation per follow-up (DTS about 0.1~mSv vs about 1.5~mSv for low-dose CT or several mSv for diagnostic CT), which matters because nodule follow-up repeats over years~\cite{rampinelli2017,mascalchi2017}. The main physical risk is a wrong measurement that delays treatment of a growing cancer or leads to an unnecessary biopsy. \emph{Mental:} living with an indeterminate nodule causes anxiety, and a falsely reassuring or falsely alarming number makes it worse.

\paragraph{Actionable solutions.} Every number is reported with a calibrated interval and category probabilities so that the clinician sees when a result is uncertain; a low-confidence flag recommends CT; and results should be explained to patients by the clinician in categories rather than raw numbers. \Cref{tab:risk} lists the main hazards.

\subsection{Social Constraint}

In the short term, DTS-based follow-up could shorten CT waiting lists and reduce travel for patients outside major cities. In the long term it could make nodule surveillance, and later screening, feasible where CT capacity is the bottleneck, improving early diagnosis where the survival gain is largest. From the patient's perspective this means less dose and cost; from the radiologist's, a measurement tool with honest uncertainty; from the health system's, cheaper follow-up that needs training and local validation. Possible negative effects are over-diagnosis if many more people are followed, and a widening gap if only private hospitals adopt the tool; both argue for public-sector deployment and guideline-based use.

\subsection{Political Constraint}

Adoption of health technology depends on public health policy and procurement. In Bangladesh, imaging equipment and software are purchased mainly through government tenders and large private hospital groups, so adoption would need evidence accepted by the health authorities and inclusion in national cancer-control plans. The use of foreign cloud computing for health data is itself a policy question, so a deployment should keep data and computation in the country.

\subsection{Sustainability}

\paragraph{Lifecycle plan.} (1) Development and verification (this project); (2) retrospective validation on real DTS with CT ground truth from a partner hospital; (3) a prospective reader study; (4) regulatory registration; (5) deployment with monitoring of measurement errors and interval coverage; and (6) periodic recalibration of the conformal layer, which needs only new calibration cases, not retraining, when scanners or populations change.

\paragraph{Three dimensions.} \emph{Environmental:} small model, low energy per measurement, no special hardware. \emph{Economic:} runs on one mid-range GPU or a low-cost cloud instance, and open-source code avoids licence fees. \emph{Social:} documented and tested code that another team can maintain, and a method that extends to other limited-angle modalities.

\subsection{Legal Considerations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Data licence:} LIDC-IDRI is distributed by TCIA under the Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 Unported licence, which allows commercial, scientific and educational use with attribution. The TCIA data usage policy forbids any attempt to identify or contact the participants and requires every publication to cite both the collection~\cite{armato2011lidc} and the TCIA repository~\cite{clark2013tcia}~\cite{tcia_policy}. We follow both requirements and made no attempt at re-identification.
  \item \textbf{Medical device regulation in Bangladesh:} medical devices are regulated by the Directorate General of Drug Administration (DGDA). The Drugs and Cosmetics Act 2023~\cite{bddca2023}, passed by Parliament in September 2023, replaced the Drugs Act 1940 and the Drugs (Control) Ordinance 1982 and includes provisions on medical devices. Registration follows the DGDA Registration Guidelines for Medical Devices~\cite{dgda2015}, which are based on the Global Harmonization Task Force guidance and define a medical device to include software intended for diagnosis or monitoring. A clinical version of MedLift-3D would therefore be a registrable medical device; implementing rules under the 2023 Act are still being developed, so the current DGDA requirements must be confirmed at the time of submission.
  \item \textbf{Other markets:} the US FDA (510(k) or De Novo), the EU Medical Device Regulation~\cite{eumdr} and the EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact}, which classes medical AI as high risk, would apply; each requires risk management~\cite{iso14971}, life-cycle processes~\cite{iec62304} and clinical evidence.
  \item \textbf{Data protection:} Bangladesh's Personal Data Protection Ordinance 2025~\cite{bdpdpo2025}, approved on 9 October 2025 and published in the official gazette on 6 November 2025, recognises citizens as the owners of their personal data, makes explicit consent mandatory before personal data is collected, stored, transferred or used, treats health records as sensitive data with stricter rules and penalties, and restricts cross-border transfers; some provisions take effect 18 months after publication. A clinical deployment would have to obtain consent, keep health data within these rules (and GDPR or HIPAA for international data), and preferably compute inside the country. The demonstration uses only de-identified public data.
  \item \textbf{Liability:} the intended use is decision support; the clinician remains responsible for the diagnosis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cultural Considerations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Language and literacy:} a deployed interface should offer Bangla and present results in plain categories, with numbers available for specialists.
  \item \textbf{Family-centred decisions:} diagnoses are often discussed with the family, so uncertainty should be explained through the clinician in a way the family can follow, not as raw probabilities.
  \item \textbf{Stigma:} lung cancer is often linked to smoking and blame; communication should avoid blame, and many patients, especially women exposed to cooking smoke, have never smoked.
  \item \textbf{Local disease patterns:} healed tuberculosis leaves calcified granulomas that resemble nodules. The model was not trained on such patients, so local validation is required before use, which also respects patients' right to a tool tested on people like them.
  \item \textbf{Trust:} many patients trust the doctor more than a machine; keeping the clinician in charge respects this.
\end{itemize}

\section{Complex Engineering Problem}

MedLift-3D is a complex engineering problem: it is ill-posed by physics, combines X-ray physics, generative AI and statistics, has conflicting requirements (dose, accuracy, honesty, cost), and has direct consequences for patients.

\paragraph{P2 -- Range of conflicting requirements.} Dose vs accuracy, filling the missing wedge vs never inventing anatomy, single numbers vs honest intervals, memory vs field of view, and detection recall vs false positives (\cref{tab:conflicts}).

\paragraph{P3 -- Depth of analysis.} We derived from first principles which quantities DTS can measure, built a 583-patient virtual trial, and analysed calibration, size-group coverage and seen/unseen content rather than image quality alone.

\paragraph{P4 -- Familiarity of issues.} No standard solution exists for calibrated 3D nodule measurement from DTS; the combination of a data-consistent prior inside a DTS prism, Gaussian mass and conformal calibration had to be designed for this problem.

\paragraph{P5 -- Extent of applicable codes.} DICOM, IEC 62304, ISO 14971, data licences and medical device regulation (\cref{tab:swstd}).

\paragraph{P6 -- Extent of stakeholder involvement (partial).} Clinical needs were taken from guidelines and supervisor feedback; direct evaluation by radiologists is future work.

\paragraph{P7 -- Interdependence.} Simulation, reconstruction, prior, Gaussians, calibration and detector depend on each other: the depth-support bug in reconstruction, for example, corrupted the training data and every downstream measurement until it was fixed.

\section{Summary}

MedLift-3D follows the software, hardware and communication standards relevant to medical imaging software and prepares for IEC 62304 and ISO 14971. Its main impacts are positive (lower dose and cost, wider access), with clear risks (wrong measurements, over-trust, untested populations) that the design mitigates through calibrated intervals, flags, clinician control and a validation plan. It meets the attributes of a complex engineering problem.
